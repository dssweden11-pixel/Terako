All About the Bloch Sphere

The Bloch sphere is a geometric representation of the quantum state of a single two-level quantum system, or qubit. This guide explains how to read the sphere, understand quantum states, use quantum gates, interpret measurements, and apply the simulator to more advanced quantum-mechanics investigations.

Understanding the Bloch Sphere

A general pure qubit state can be written as:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩

Instead of thinking only in terms of complex amplitudes, the Bloch sphere converts the state into a direction in three-dimensional space.

The angle θ determines how far the state is between the north and south poles. The angle φ determines its direction around the sphere.

North Pole: |0⟩
South Pole: |1⟩
Equator: Equal-probability superpositions

For example, the state

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩) / √2

lies on the equator. The state

|-⟩ = (|0⟩ - |1⟩) / √2

lies on the opposite side of the equator.

Components of the Bloch Vector

A pure qubit can also be represented by a three-dimensional Bloch vector:

r = (sinθ cosφ, sinθ sinφ, cosθ)

Its X, Y, and Z components have a direct quantum-mechanical interpretation:

x = ⟨σx⟩
y = ⟨σy⟩
z = ⟨σz⟩

These are expectation values of the Pauli operators. This means that the geometry of the Bloch sphere is connected to quantities that can be determined experimentally.

Pure States and Mixed States

Pure States

A pure state represents a completely specified quantum state. Pure states lie on the surface of the Bloch sphere.

|r| = 1

Mixed States

Real quantum systems may interact with their environment or contain uncertainty in their preparation. These systems can require a density matrix rather than a single state vector.

ρ = ½(I + r · σ)

Mixed states lie inside the sphere:

|r| < 1

At the exact center of the sphere,

r = 0

ρ = I/2

which represents the maximally mixed single-qubit state.

Quantum Gates

Quantum gates transform quantum states. For a single qubit, unitary transformations can be interpreted geometrically as rotations of the Bloch sphere.

X Gate

X = [ 0   1 ]
    [ 1   0 ]

The X gate exchanges |0⟩ and |1⟩ and corresponds to a 180° rotation around the X-axis.

X|0⟩ = |1⟩

Y Gate

Y = [ 0   -i ]
    [ i     0 ]

The Y gate produces a 180° rotation around the Y-axis. It changes both the computational-basis amplitudes and their relative phase.

Z Gate

Z = [ 1    0 ]
    [ 0   -1 ]

The Z gate corresponds to a 180° rotation around the Z-axis. It leaves |0⟩ unchanged while changing the sign of the |1⟩ amplitude.

Z|0⟩ = |0⟩
Z|1⟩ = -|1⟩

This demonstrates something important: a quantum state can change even when its probabilities of measuring 0 and 1 do not change. Relative phase contains quantum information.

Hadamard Gate

H = (1/√2) [ 1    1 ]
           [ 1   -1 ]

The Hadamard gate can move a computational-basis state into an equal superposition.

H|0⟩ = |+⟩

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2
Simulator Instruction Manual

Step 1 — Choose a State

Begin with a known quantum state. For example, start at |0⟩. The Bloch vector should point toward the north pole.

Step 2 — Apply a Gate

Select one of the available quantum gates. Watch how the Bloch vector moves around the sphere.

Step 3 — Identify the Rotation

Do not only look at the final position. Determine which axis the vector rotated around and how the gate changed the quantum state.

Step 4 — Combine Gates

Try sequences of gates instead of applying only one.

|0⟩ → H → |+⟩

|+⟩ → Z → |-⟩

|-⟩ → H → |1⟩

Therefore:

HZH|0⟩ = |1⟩

Step 5 — Compare With the Mathematics

Calculate the transformation using matrices and compare your mathematical prediction with the simulator.

Quantum Measurement

For the state

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩

measurement in the computational basis gives:

P(0) = cos²(θ/2)

P(1) = sin²(θ/2)

Notice that φ does not appear in these probabilities. Measuring only in the Z basis therefore cannot reveal all information about the relative phase.

Measurements along different axes can be used to investigate different components of the Bloch vector.

Using the Bloch Sphere for Research

The Bloch sphere becomes much more powerful when it is used as a mathematical laboratory rather than only as a picture of a qubit.

Study Arbitrary Rotations

Investigate rotations around different axes and compare their matrix representations with their geometric effects.

U = Rz(α) Ry(β) Rx(γ)

Investigate Gate Errors

Real quantum gates are imperfect. You can model a small rotation error by replacing

Rx(θ)

with

Rx(θ + ε)

and studying how the final state changes as the error ε increases.

Study Decoherence

An advanced version of the simulator can represent density matrices and allow the Bloch vector to move inside the sphere. This makes it possible to visualize effects such as dephasing, amplitude damping, and loss of coherence.

Quantum State Tomography

In an experiment, the state may not be known beforehand. Measurements can instead be used to reconstruct it.

r = (⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩, ⟨Z⟩)

From the reconstructed Bloch vector, the density matrix can be determined:

ρ = ½(I + r · σ)

This turns the simulator into a useful introduction to quantum-state characterization and experimental quantum information.

Limitations of the Bloch Sphere

The standard Bloch sphere describes a single qubit extremely well, but it cannot directly visualize an arbitrary multi-qubit state.

A two-qubit pure state already lives in a four-dimensional complex Hilbert space, and entanglement contains correlations that cannot be represented simply by assigning each qubit an independent Bloch vector.

The Bloch sphere should therefore be viewed as a powerful geometric representation of a single two-level quantum system and as an introduction to the larger mathematical structure of quantum mechanics.
